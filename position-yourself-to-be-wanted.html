I Am Ready to Open It

A Career Reflection

Position Yourself to Be Wanted

Ask the questions that create opportunity—and develop the skills, habits, and character that make you difficult to ignore.

Many young people are worried about the future. They look at unemployment, rising living costs, competition for jobs, and the advantage that family connections seem to give some people. These concerns are real. Not everyone begins life with the same access to money, networks, elite schools, or influential people.

But there is one powerful truth that should give every young person hope:

You can position yourself to be wanted.

You may not be able to choose your family background. You may not control the state of the economy. You may not have a godfather who can make one phone call and secure a job for you. But you can control your attitude, your diligence, your learning, your character, and the value you bring to every opportunity.

Over time, these qualities become visible. And when they become visible, doors begin to open.

You can position yourself in such a way that you cannot be ignored.

That does not happen by chance. It begins when you become intentional about your life, your work, your skills, and your future. You must learn to ask yourself important questions—again and again.

Many people remain stuck not only because opportunities are scarce, but because they do not pause to ask the questions that can reveal a new direction. They spend their time complaining about what they do not have instead of examining what they can learn, build, improve, and become.

The future often changes for the person who begins to ask better questions.

Ask Yourself the Right Questions

  • What jobs, skills, and professions are currently in high demand—and how can I qualify for them?
  • What problems are employers, businesses, and communities struggling to solve?
  • What can I learn that will increase my value and help me earn higher pay?
  • How can I build an income that can withstand inflation and rising living costs?
  • Which skills are likely to remain useful even as technology and industries change?
  • What is working for people who are succeeding in the field I want to enter?
  • What is not working, and what mistakes should I avoid?
  • Who has overcome circumstances similar to mine successfully—and what did they do differently?
  • Who am I? What are my strengths, interests, values, and natural abilities?
  • Where am I going, and what kind of person must I become to get there?
  • What is the worst that can happen if I try?
  • What do I truly stand to lose if I take a well-considered step forward?

Questions, questions, questions.

Do not be afraid of questions. Questions are not a sign that you are lost; they are often evidence that you are thinking. The right question can reveal an opportunity you have ignored, a skill you need to acquire, a person you need to learn from, or a decision you have been postponing.

At some point, if you keep asking sincere questions, researching, learning, observing, and acting on the answers, you will answer one question that leads to your breakthrough.

“What valuable skill can I combine with my degree?”

That was a question that changed the direction of my own career.

I had studied Computer Science, but while serving at UBA, a manager noticed my diligence and gave me advice I had not considered. He told me that the industry needed people who could combine Computer Science with accounting. He encouraged me to pursue ICAN.

That insight gave me a new direction. I made a difficult decision: I left a high-paying job at UBA and accepted a role in an accounting firm for almost one-third of the salary. It was not an easy decision, but it gave me the practical experience and environment I needed to qualify as a Chartered Accountant.

Two years later, I became a Chartered Accountant.

At that point, I had a combination that many employers wanted: Computer Science and professional accountancy. It no longer mattered whether I had a godfather or not. I had developed qualifications, discipline, and a valuable combination of skills that made me more useful to employers. That positioning helped me rise through the corporate ladder very quickly.

Your breakthrough may also begin with one answer. But that answer will usually come only after you develop the discipline to ask the right questions.

Turn Questions Into Action

However, questions alone are not enough. You must do something with the answers you receive.

If you discover that data analysis is in demand, find out which tools employers require and begin learning them. If you identify a professional qualification that will strengthen your career, create a realistic plan to pursue it. If you see someone whose journey inspires you, study their path and learn from their discipline—not merely their success.

Use this simple pattern:

01AskAsk a serious question about your future.
02ResearchSeek reliable answers and practical insight.
03ChooseSelect one realistic next step.
04ActBe consistent, even when progress feels slow.
05ReviewLearn from results and ask better questions again.

For example, instead of saying, “There are no jobs,” ask:

“Which employers are hiring people with skills I can realistically learn in the next six to twelve months?”

Then identify the skills they require. Study job descriptions. Speak to people already working in those fields. Take a relevant course. Practise with real projects. Build evidence of what you can do. Then begin applying.

Instead of saying, “I do not have connections,” ask:

“How can I become so skilled, diligent, and reliable that people will be willing to recommend me?”

Then let your work, attitude, integrity, and consistency become your introduction.

Connections may help some people gain access to a room. But competence, character, and results are what make people want to keep you in that room—and recommend you for bigger opportunities.

Make Reflection a Habit

Set aside time every week to think seriously about your life and career. Write down the questions on your mind. Do not leave your future to random thoughts, social-media trends, or the opinions of every person around you.

Questions That Will Shape My Future

Keep a notebook with this title. Each week, write down:

  • One career question.
  • One skill question.
  • One income question.
  • One personal-development question.
  • One action you will take before the week ends.

Over time, you will begin to see patterns. You will become clearer about your direction. You will stop drifting. You will begin to make decisions based on knowledge, purpose, and preparation rather than fear.

The person who keeps learning, keeps asking, keeps improving, and keeps acting will eventually become difficult to ignore.

Read slowly. Reflect honestly. Take one action.
