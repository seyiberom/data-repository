Career Starter Guide

What Employers Want to See in Your CV

A practical guide for final-year students, graduates and early-career professionals who want their CV to earn serious attention.

See the essentials Take the 20-second test

Employers are not looking for a perfect CV. They want evidence that you can do the job, learn quickly and behave professionally. Your CV should make it easy for them to see why you are relevant to their vacancy—not simply tell your life story.

The essentials

Six things employers want to see

1

Clear contact details

Your name, professional phone number, professional email, location and a strong LinkedIn profile where relevant.

2

A clear direction

A short summary that shows who you are, the role you want and the value you can offer.

3

Relevant skills

Technical and people skills that match the role, supported by real evidence in your experience.

4

Evidence of responsibility

Internships, projects, volunteering, leadership, NYSC, freelance work or community service that prove you can contribute.

5

Results and achievements

Examples of what you did well, what changed and how you created value for people or a team.

6

Fit for this job

A CV tailored to the employer’s actual job description, not one generic document sent everywhere.

Start well

1. Make contact details easy to find

Include

  • Full name
  • Professional phone number
  • Professional email address
  • Location, such as Lagos, Abuja, Ibadan or “Available to relocate”
  • LinkedIn profile link, if it is well prepared

Usually leave out

  • Religion, state of origin and marital status
  • Full home address and date of birth
  • Primary-school history
  • A photograph, unless specifically requested
  • Unprofessional email addresses
Make your direction clear

2. Write a useful professional summary

Use three or four lines to explain who you are, the role you want, your strongest relevant skills and the value you can bring.

Example:

Final-year Economics student with experience supporting student events, handling enquiries and coordinating teams. Strong written communication, Excel and customer-service skills. Seeking an entry-level customer-support or operations role where I can learn quickly, support clients professionally and contribute to an organised team.

Avoid generic claims: “I am hardworking, honest and a team player.” Show these qualities through examples instead.
Show the skills

3. Include skills employers can use

Strong applicants show both technical/job skills and transferable people skills.

Technical / job skillsTransferable / people skills
Microsoft Excel or Google SheetsClear communication
PowerPoint and report writingTeamwork
Data entry and record keepingProblem-solving
CRM or customer-support toolsReliability and time management
Research, presentations and social-media supportAdaptability and professional conduct
Important: Do not only list a skill. Show where you used it. For example, if you say “Excel,” describe the tracker, report or record you created.
Experience counts

4. Show evidence—even if you have not held a full-time job

Graduates are not expected to have ten years of experience. Employers want proof that you have taken responsibility, worked with people or completed useful work.

Include relevant experience such as:

  • Internships and industrial training
  • NYSC service or NYSC status
  • Volunteering and community service
  • Student leadership and campus projects
  • Research projects and practical coursework
  • Small business, freelance or tutoring work
  • Event planning and social-media support

How to write it

Do not merely state your title. Describe the responsibility, the action you took and the result.

Weak: Assisted with an event.

Better: Coordinated registration for a 200-person student event, responded to participant enquiries and maintained an attendance record using Google Sheets.

Show results

5. Turn duties into achievements

Employers want to know not only what you were asked to do, but how well you performed.

Action verb + what you did + how / with whom + result

Examples with numbers

  • Responded to more than 30 customer enquiries weekly through WhatsApp and phone, helping reduce unanswered requests during peak periods.
  • Led a five-person student team to organise a career seminar attended by 150 students.
  • Supported onboarding for 25 new volunteers by preparing welcome materials and answering questions.

Examples without numbers

  • Created a step-by-step guide that simplified registration for new participants.
  • Resolved common participant enquiries by preparing a clear FAQ document.
  • Improved the organisation of project files by introducing a shared digital folder system.
Education and focus

6. Present your education and training properly

Include

  • University/polytechnic and course of study
  • Expected graduation date if you are still studying
  • Degree class or CGPA if it strengthens your application
  • Relevant courses, certifications and practical training
  • Final-year project where it relates to the role

Example

B.Sc. Business Administration — University of [Name]
Expected graduation: 2026
Relevant coursework: Customer Relationship Management, Business Communication, Operations Management.

The most important rule

Tailor your CV to the vacancy

Do not use one unchanged CV for every job. Read the job advert and identify what the employer repeatedly asks for: skills, tasks, software, qualifications and behaviours.

Example: For a customer-service role, the employer may want communication, complaint resolution, empathy, record keeping and computer skills. Your CV should truthfully show those qualities through your summary, skills and experience.
Avoid these mistakes

What employers do not want

  • A generic CV sent unchanged to every employer
  • Long paragraphs that are difficult to scan
  • Spelling and grammar mistakes
  • False qualifications or invented experience
  • Vague claims with no evidence
  • Irrelevant personal information
  • An unprofessional email address
  • Heavy graphics or too many colours for conventional applications
  • A CV longer than two pages for a student or early-career applicant
  • Dead links, missing contact details or outdated information
A simple structure

Use this CV format

FULL NAME
Phone | Professional email | Location | LinkedIn

PROFESSIONAL SUMMARY
Three to four targeted lines.

KEY SKILLS
Six to ten skills relevant to the role.

EDUCATION
University/polytechnic, programme, graduation date, relevant coursework/project.

EXPERIENCE / PROJECTS / VOLUNTEERING
Organisation | Role | Dates

CERTIFICATIONS / TRAINING
Relevant short courses, tools or credentials.

LEADERSHIP / ADDITIONAL INFORMATION
Student leadership, relevant volunteering, languages or interests.

The 20-second CV test

Before you send your CV, ask yourself: If a recruiter spends only 20 seconds on this page, can they see the role I want, the skills I offer and at least two examples that show I can contribute?
